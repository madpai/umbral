# UMBRAL Design Philosophy

> A person’s life is finite. Their influence is not.

This document is UMBRAL’s creative constitution.

It defines the principles by which features, systems, stories, technologies, and worldbuilding decisions are evaluated. It does not describe individual mechanics or establish fictional lore. Instead, it protects the identity of UMBRAL as those details are created.

`UMBRAL_VISION.md` explains why the project exists.

`GAME_DESIGN_DOCUMENT.md` describes the game that should emerge from that vision.

This document establishes the standards that both must obey.

When a proposed feature conflicts with these principles, the feature must change or be rejected.

---

## The Central Question

UMBRAL asks:

> What does it mean to leave something behind?

The answer may take many forms:

- Knowledge
- Relationships
- Roads
- Settlements
- Works of craft
- Traditions
- Institutions
- Conflicts
- Songs
- Ruins
- Mistakes
- Memories

UMBRAL is a world where existence is measured not only by what someone possesses or accomplishes during a lifetime, but by the traces that remain after they are gone.

Legacy is not a secondary theme.

It is the foundation from which the world and its systems are built.

---

## The Oldest Truth

The world is older than the player, and it is not waiting for them.

UMBRAL does not begin when a player arrives. Its peoples have histories, its landscapes bear scars, and its institutions have purposes independent of the player’s presence.

The player enters an existing world.

They may learn from it, participate in it, protect it, damage it, transform it, and leave something within it. They do not automatically become its center.

The world is indifferent to any individual, but it is responsive to collective action.

---

## The Ten Foundations

### 1. The World — Permanence

UMBRAL is a world of permanence.

Things matter because they persist.

Roads are worn by generations. Buildings survive their builders. Forests remember fires. Settlements inherit the consequences of earlier decisions. Ruins are evidence rather than decoration.

Persistence does not require every object or event to remain forever. It requires the world to preserve meaningful consequences.

Some traces endure physically.

Some survive through culture.

Some become history.

Some become myth.

Some disappear, leaving only the shape of their absence.

The world should feel accumulated rather than assembled.

---

### 2. Time — Pressure

Time is not merely a resource or visual cycle.

Time is pressure.

People age. Materials decay. Landscapes change. Institutions drift from their founding purposes. Settlements grow, decline, adapt, or disappear. Knowledge is preserved, distorted, rediscovered, or lost.

The world must not feel frozen until a player interacts with it.

Change may occur at different scales and speeds, but UMBRAL should always carry the sense that history is continuing.

Time gives decisions weight because opportunities do not remain unchanged forever.

---

### 3. Life — Participation

Life is defined by participation.

To live is to affect the world while being affected by it.

This principle applies to people, communities, animals, ecosystems, and civilizations. Living things are not isolated entities placed into a static environment. They exist through relationships.

Every life consumes, contributes, adapts, resists, and leaves traces.

The value of life is not determined only by intelligence, combat ability, status, or usefulness to the player.

The world is alive because its inhabitants participate in one another’s existence.

---

### 4. Death — Legacy

Death ends action.

It does not erase consequence.

UMBRAL should not treat death merely as a punishment, inconvenience, or temporary interruption. Its deeper purpose is to establish finitude.

Lives matter because they end.

What remains afterward gives those lives continuity:

- A structure
- A craft
- A family
- A reputation
- A debt
- A discovery
- A damaged landscape
- A protected community
- A story that survives its subject

Death creates the boundary that makes legacy meaningful.

The world must not reset simply because an individual is gone.

---

### 5. Memory — Imperfect Continuity

The world remembers, but it does not remember perfectly.

Memory may be preserved through people, places, records, rituals, objects, landscapes, institutions, and stories. Every method of preservation has limits.

Some memories fade.

Some are deliberately erased.

Some are misunderstood.

Some become mythology.

Some become lies repeated so often that they shape reality through belief and action.

History is not an infallible database. It is accumulated, contested memory.

This gives genuine importance to witnesses, historians, archivists, monuments, oral traditions, artifacts, and ruins.

Discovery is not only about finding places.

It is also about recovering meaning.

---

### 6. Power — The Capacity to Change

Power is the capacity to change the world.

It is not synonymous with violence, rank, wealth, or damage output.

A warrior may be powerful.

So may a builder who keeps a settlement alive, a cartographer who reveals a safe passage, a craftsperson whose work survives generations, a teacher who preserves knowledge, or an organizer who gives a community direction.

Different forms of power create different kinds of consequences.

UMBRAL should recognize influence, responsibility, knowledge, creation, coordination, and stewardship as meaningful expressions of power.

Combat is one form of influence, not the universal measure of worth.

---

### 7. Consequence — Reality Is Trustworthy

Actions have consequences.

They may not be immediate.

They may not be predictable.

They may not be fair.

They may be hidden for a time.

But they exist.

UMBRAL must avoid casually invalidating meaningful events through arbitrary resets, convenient reversals, or revelations that make prior choices irrelevant.

The world may contain uncertainty, deception, disputed history, and incomplete understanding. Reality itself, however, must be treated with integrity.

Cause and effect are fundamental.

When the world changes, that change must be respected.

---

### 8. Civilization — Defiance Against Oblivion

People build civilizations because they do not want everything to vanish with them.

A civilization is more than population, territory, architecture, or government. It is a collective attempt to preserve meaning across generations.

People gather to survive, but they remain together to remember, create, teach, protect, organize, and continue.

Cities are memories built from material.

Institutions are promises extended into the future.

Traditions are conversations between the living and the dead.

Every civilization should express what its people believe deserves to outlast them.

Its failures should reveal what it was unwilling or unable to preserve.

---

### 9. Conflict — Competing Futures

Conflict emerges from competing visions of the future.

UMBRAL should not depend on simplistic divisions between universally good and universally evil societies. Most people and factions should understand themselves as protecting, restoring, improving, or preparing the world.

They may disagree about:

- What deserves preservation
- What must be sacrificed
- Who should bear a cost
- Who may exercise power
- Which memories are legitimate
- What the future should become

Moral complexity does not require moral emptiness.

Cruelty remains cruelty. Exploitation remains exploitation. Understanding a motive does not excuse its consequences.

Conflict becomes meaningful when participants want futures that cannot all coexist.

---

### 10. Hope — Change Is Possible

Hope exists because the world can become better.

Improvement is not guaranteed.

History does not naturally bend toward justice.

Sacrifice does not always succeed.

Good intentions do not prevent harm.

Even so, action matters.

People do not require prophecy, destiny, or divine selection to become significant. Ordinary lives can produce extraordinary consequences when effort accumulates across time and community.

Players matter not because the universe declares them uniquely important, but because participation creates influence.

Hope is the belief that what someone leaves behind may be better than what they inherited.

---

## The World Is the Game

UMBRAL is not a collection of disconnected activities placed against a decorative setting.

The world is the game.

Exploration, conflict, trade, construction, governance, knowledge, ecology, crafting, and social life must reinforce one another through the world they inhabit.

Systems should not exist only because the genre traditionally includes them.

A system belongs when it deepens the player’s relationship with the world and with the people who inhabit it.

The goal is not to maximize the number of features.

The goal is to create a coherent place.

---

## The Player Is a Participant

The player is not automatically:

- The chosen one
- The center of history
- The only competent person
- The solution to every problem
- Entitled to every secret
- Guaranteed permanent control
- Exempt from consequence

Players should be capable of extraordinary influence, but that influence must emerge through participation.

They enter communities they did not create.

They encounter knowledge they do not yet understand.

They depend on others.

They inherit consequences.

They decide what to preserve, what to change, and what they are willing to risk.

Significance is earned through relationship with the world.

---

## Legacy Is the Deepest Progression

Progression in UMBRAL should expand a player’s capacity to understand, participate in, and influence the world.

Numerical growth may exist, but it is not enough by itself.

A mature character, community, or institution should possess greater:

- Knowledge
- Responsibility
- Reach
- Trust
- Craft
- Influence
- Stewardship
- Ability to create lasting change

The deepest measure of progression is not what a player can carry.

It is what can continue because they were there.

---

## Persistence Is a Responsibility

Persistence is not a marketing claim.

It is a design responsibility.

A persistent world must decide:

- What deserves to remain
- What should decay
- What can be restored
- What should be remembered abstractly
- What may be forgotten
- How changes remain legible
- How new participants inherit an altered world
- How the world avoids becoming permanently exhausted or inaccessible

Not every footprint must last forever.

Meaningful consequences must.

Persistence should create history, not clutter.

---

## Systems Must Produce Stories

UMBRAL should provide systems through which stories emerge rather than relying entirely on authored sequences.

A road becomes meaningful because people needed it.

A settlement becomes memorable because people sustained it.

An object becomes important because of who made it, used it, lost it, recovered it, or inherited it.

A conflict becomes history because it changed relationships and places.

Systems should create conditions in which players and inhabitants generate stories with real context and consequence.

Authored narrative may guide, reveal, and deepen the world, but it should not make the rest of the world feel inert.

---

## Design Through Questions

Feature design should begin with the world-facing question rather than the conventional genre label.

Do not begin with:

> How should crafting work?

Begin with:

> How can making something allow a person to leave value behind?

Do not begin with:

> How should player conflict work?

Begin with:

> How can conflict change people, relationships, places, and history?

Do not begin with:

> How should housing work?

Begin with:

> How can a place acquire identity, continuity, and memory?

Do not begin with:

> What classes should exist?

Begin with:

> What forms of contribution, mastery, responsibility, and identity does this world need?

Mechanics are answers.

They are not the questions.

---

## The Coherence Test

Every major feature or design proposal should be evaluated against the following questions:

1. Does it deepen the world rather than merely add content?
2. Does it reinforce permanence, consequence, memory, participation, or legacy?
3. Does it respect the world’s existence beyond the individual player?
4. Does it create meaningful relationships between systems?
5. Can it produce stories that belong specifically to UMBRAL?
6. Does it treat non-combat contribution as genuinely valuable where appropriate?
7. Does it preserve cause and effect?
8. Does it create responsibility alongside power?
9. Does it provide opportunities for collective influence?
10. Does it help the world feel accumulated, inhabited, and alive?

A proposal does not need to satisfy every question equally.

It must, however, strengthen the identity of UMBRAL rather than dilute it.

## The Dual Design Test

Every major proposal must answer two questions:

1. Does this make the world more coherent?
2. Does this create interesting gameplay?

A proposal is not ready when either answer is no.

Lore cannot compensate for weak mechanics.

Mechanical novelty cannot justify incoherent worldbuilding.

Dialogue is optional engagement, not the sole delivery mechanism. Themes should
be expressed through consequences, spaces, systems, routines, and player
choices.

Players should be able to create personal stories without consuming authored
narrative.

Immediate feel, feedback, responsiveness, and clarity are production
requirements, not polish reserved for the end.

---

## Warning Signs

A proposal should be challenged when its primary justification is:

- Other games have it
- Players expect it
- It creates more content
- It increases scale
- It follows a current trend
- It produces a larger number
- It removes all inconvenience
- It makes every activity equally accessible to every character
- It allows consequences to be ignored
- It treats the world as a backdrop
- It makes the player universally central
- It adds complexity without deepening relationships

These arguments do not automatically invalidate a feature.

They are insufficient on their own.

---

## What UMBRAL Will Avoid

UMBRAL will avoid:

- Feature accumulation without coherence
- Generic fantasy elements included without purpose
- Worlds that wait motionless for the player
- Chosen-one structures as the default form of significance
- Progression measured only through numerical superiority
- Combat as the sole legitimate expression of power
- Settlements that function only as service menus
- Crafting without social, material, or historical context
- Conflict without enduring consequences
- Persistence that preserves clutter rather than meaning
- Lore disconnected from lived systems
- Systems that erase responsibility
- Scale pursued at the expense of depth
- Trend chasing that weakens the project’s identity
- Resets that casually invalidate history
- Complexity mistaken for depth

---

## Creative Authority

This philosophy is intended to constrain the project.

That constraint is valuable.

UMBRAL should not attempt to contain every good idea. It should contain the ideas that belong together.

Future documents may refine how these principles are expressed. They must not quietly abandon them.

Changes to this philosophy should be deliberate, documented, and treated as changes to the identity of the project rather than ordinary edits.

---

## The Promise

UMBRAL promises a world that existed before the player, changes while they inhabit it, and carries something of them after they are gone.

A player’s life within the world may be finite.

Their influence does not have to be.
